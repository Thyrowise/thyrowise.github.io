How to choose a thyroid tracker app: 7 things that actually matter.

Most thyroid apps look alike on the store page. These are the differences that decide whether you still have your data in three years.

1. Where does the data live?

This is the first question, not the last one. An app with an account keeps your thyroid history on someone else's server, which means a company failure, a policy change or a breach is a thing that can happen to your medical record. An app that stores on-device cannot lose your data to any of those — but it also cannot get it back for you if you lose the phone without a backup.

Neither answer is wrong. Choosing without knowing which one you picked is.

2. Does it hold your target range, or a generic one?

A typical adult TSH reference range is about 0.4–4.0 mIU/L, but plenty of people on levothyroxine are being steered to a narrower target. An app that colours everything under 4.0 as "normal" is useless to someone told to stay under 2.5. Look for a personal band you can set yourself. More on ranges and why labs disagree.

3. Are dose changes first-class objects?

A TSH value on its own says very little. A TSH value six weeks after a dose went from 75 to 88 micrograms says something. If dose changes are just free text in a notes field, the app cannot line them up against results — and the single most useful view, the trend with changes marked on it, is impossible.

4. How long does a normal day take?

Daily logging is only sustained if it costs seconds. One-tap symptom chips get kept; a form with sliders and a free-text box gets abandoned in a month, and one abandoned month is most of the value gone when the review interval is six.

5. Can you get the data out?

PDF for the appointment, CSV for yourself. Export is also your exit: an app you can leave is an app you can safely commit to. If there is no export, every entry you make increases the cost of ever switching.

6. Does it cite anything?

Any app stating a reference range, a retest interval or an absorption rule is making a medical claim. Check whether it says where the claim came from. Named sources — the American Thyroid Association, NIDDK, MedlinePlus — mean someone did the work and you can check it. No sources means you are trusting a developer's memory.

7. Does it tell you things, or guess at them?

"Fatigue on 19 of the last 30 days, against 8 in the 30 before" is a count you can verify and take to a doctor. A wellness score out of 100, or an AI paragraph about what your labs suggest, is a black box in a domain where the treatment decision is not yours to make. Prefer the count.
